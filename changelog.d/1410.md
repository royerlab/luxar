#### LOD coverage metric no longer drifts with viewport aspect ratio

The auto-selecting `kind=lod` group picked a coarser level than intended on a
wide monitor, reproducing #1361's opening-framing blur at aspect ratios beyond
roughly 16:9. The cause: `calculateCameraDistance` fits the camera to the
VERTICAL field of view for any viewport aspect >= 1 (so the framing distance is
independent of width), while the coverage metric normalised the projected bbox
diagonal by the viewport's DIAGONAL — which keeps growing with width regardless.
So the framing did not widen with the canvas but the metric's denominator did,
and a shape's raw coverage ratio fell as `raw(aspect) = raw(1)·√2 / hypot(aspect,
1)`. Measured before the fix: a cube's opening-framing metric fell from 3.43 at
1:1 to 1.31 at 32:9, and thinner shapes (a flat pancake, a cloud elongated in the
image plane) dropped below the finest-level threshold entirely on an ultrawide
canvas.

The fix normalises by the viewport's **fitted screen axis**
(`min(viewport.width, viewport.height)`) instead of its diagonal — exactly the
extent `calculateCameraDistance` fits in both aspect regimes (the vertical
extent for aspect >= 1, the horizontal one for aspect < 1). This is provably
exact for aspect >= 1: camera distance there has no aspect dependence at all, so
the projected pixel diagonal is EXACTLY proportional to the viewport height,
independent of both aspect and absolute viewport size (verified to 12
significant digits). The viewer's `FILL_FACTOR` anchor moves from `0.25` to
`0.5` in the same change, chosen so the metric's VALUE is preserved (within
~2%, measured) at the mainstream 16:9 reference — every existing
`coverage_fraction` threshold, hysteresis band, and cross-fade band keeps its
meaning. Python's `MAX_COVERAGE_FRACTION` stays `4.0`, re-expressed as the new
`SCREEN_FILL_DIAGONAL_RATIO / FILL_FACTOR` (a screen-filling object's projected
diagonal is no longer exactly the fitted axis, but `~2`x it at the 16:9
reference the constant is anchored at — `1.41`x at 1:1 and `2.57`x at a 21:9
panel, hence the trade-off below).

Trade-off, deliberately accepted: `MAX_COVERAGE_FRACTION` is now aspect-EXACT
only near aspect ratio √3 ≈ 1.73 (it was aspect-exact everywhere under the old
diagonal normalisation). A partition-bound ladder's finest level — the
fills-screen anchor used by tiled layers (`adaptive`, `overview`) — therefore
needs a somewhat larger projected size to reach it at square-ish/portrait-ish
aspect ratios than before this fix (measured: `x1.41` at 1:1, `x1.20` at 4:3,
`x1.06` at 16:10, `~x1.0` at 16:9) and a somewhat smaller one at ultrawide
ratios (`x0.78` at a real 21:9 panel, `x0.54` at 32:9). This is the intentional flip side of
making the WHOLE-OBJECT anchor (`coverage_fractions`, reached by a normal
full-frame view) aspect-exact instead — that anchor is what #1410's blur
actually hit, while a tiled layer's per-tile anchor was already a coarse
heuristic.

Also known and unchanged here: `updateCameraAspect` never re-fits the camera
distance on a window resize (it only preserves the vertical fov / ortho
extent), so with height held fixed, narrowing a viewport is not symmetric
with widening one: the new metric only overtakes the old past a crossover
around aspect ~0.56 (506x900 for a 1600x900 baseline) — down to that point
the new normalisation is actually LESS inflated than the old one (1600x900 ->
500x900, aspect 0.56: `x1.80` new vs `x1.78` old, already a near-wash), and
only below it does narrowing inflate the metric faster than before
(1600x900 -> 200x900, aspect 0.22: `x4.50` new vs `x1.99` old). Widening a
viewport — the actual #1410 symptom — is exactly stable under the fix (proven
above); narrowing one past that crossover without a re-fit is a pre-existing,
separate limitation this fix does not address.

Not fixed here: a cloud elongated along the VIEW axis (e.g. `1×1×100`) still
measures a near-zero metric, because `calculateCameraDistance` sizes the
distance from the largest dimension even when that dimension barely
contributes to the projected AABB — a camera-framing issue, not a
normalisation one.
