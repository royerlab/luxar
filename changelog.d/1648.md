#### Adaptive DPR now acts in the regime it was built for

Adaptive resolution was structurally inert below about 2.9 fps — exactly the regime a
software rasterizer (headless Chromium's SwiftShader, a machine without a usable GPU)
puts a heavy gsplat scene into. Two mechanisms conspired. The frame-gap reset, meant to
throw away a one-off stall (a GC pause, a synchronous decode, an idle resume) before it
poisons an otherwise-healthy window, fired on _every_ frame once every interval exceeded
its 350 ms threshold, clearing the sample window and pushing the evaluation clock forward
so the control loop never ran at all. And the 1-second sliding window dropped the previous
frame as too old before the next one arrived, leaving a single sample and an undefined
frame rate. A measured 116-second episode at 0.48 fps rendered at full native pixel ratio
from beginning to end.

A long interval is now classified by comparison rather than by an absolute threshold: it
is dead time only if it clears the 350 ms floor _and_ is strictly more than four times the
median of the four intervals that preceded it. A 5-second gap in a 60 fps stream is a 300×
outlier and is still thrown away; a 2-second interval in a stream whose recent intervals
are all around 2 seconds is simply the frame rate, and its samples are kept so the manager
scales down. The interval being judged is deliberately not part of the median it is judged
against — mixing it in makes the test partly self-referential, and after a session boundary
that decided the verdict: with a two-sample memory the candidate was half of it, so a
5-second shader-compile stall one frame into a session measured as "the frame rate" and the
manager reduced the pixel ratio and armed a probe on a window holding nothing but dead
time. That is the ordinary startup and idle-resume path.

The cadence memory survives the reset it triggers, which is what makes it converge — a
scene that genuinely slows down costs one or two misread intervals before adaptation
resumes — and is cleared only where the frame stream itself breaks (display change, pause,
disable, dispose). A scene-content change is deliberately not one of those: frames keep
arriving at the rate they were arriving at, and a cold memory falls back to the absolute
threshold alone, which misreads the very next ordinary interval of a slow loop as dead
time — which in turn threw the freshly restarted window away a second time and voided the
probe in flight.

The evaluation clock is no longer pinned to the stalling frame's own timestamp, only to at
most one evaluation interval before it, and a tick that found no samples at all no longer
consumes the interval budget. Without both, an outlier hitch recurring more often than the
500 ms evaluation interval re-pinned the clock before it could ever mature and the loop
never evaluated: measured over 43 seconds, a repeating 16.7/16.7/400 ms cadence (about 6.9
frames a second as perceived) produced zero evaluations with a frame rate to judge, and so
did 8×16.7+360 ms; they now produce about fifty and eighty. What those evaluations can
_see_ is a separate limit, described at the end.

With per-frame LOD churn at half a frame per second the pixel ratio used to walk
unratified from 2.0 all the way down to the floor of 0.5 without ever learning anything.
Two things stop that now. A content change no longer voids an in-flight probe when the loop
is too slow to run a replacement experiment (below roughly two frames per probe window,
every armed probe was voided before it could ever be judged), and the verdict such a probe
finally produces is treated as _confounded_: its baseline was measured on the old content
and its settle sample on the new one, so it says nothing about what the pixel-ratio change
did. A per-frame swap to a finer, heavier level reads as a rejection, and acting on that
would push the pixel ratio back _up_ — adding pixels to a scene that just got heavier — and
pin a 30-second floor that blocks all scale-down; a swap to a coarser level reads as an
acceptance and would credit the coarsening to the pixel-ratio step, wiping the
rejection-backoff streak. So a confounded probe may only conclude "stop here for now": the
reduction stands, because fewer pixels never hurt a stuttering loop, and the walk is held
by a provisional floor carrying the 5-second content-change TTL rather than the 30-second
one, until content settles and a probe can be judged again. Measured at 0.5 fps with a
content change every frame, the pixel ratio now stops two steps in — 0.81 of a native 1.0,
1.62 of a native 2.0 — and learns nothing from the churn, where the churn-free version of
the same scene settles at 0.90 with a floor of 0.81 (native 1) or at 1.00 with a floor of
0.90 (native 2). The two paths are not equivalent and are not expected to be: at native 2
the churn-free path lands on exactly 1.0 because the sustained-distress ceiling demotion
fires, and a content change clears the estimator's uniform-low plateau clock (the new
content deserves fresh evidence), so under churn that demotion never comes. The FPS window's
retention floor keeps two samples regardless of age, so the estimate stays defined slower
than the window itself.

Behaviour at healthy frame rates is unchanged, and an isolated long stall — one, or up to
two in a row — is still discarded outright. By the third in a row half the cadence memory
is dead time, so it is absorbed as the frame rate; there is no way to tell the two apart
from timestamps alone, since three five-second gaps in a row genuinely is a fifth of a
frame per second for fifteen seconds. A cadence that has only just been reclassified is
therefore untrusted for a couple of intervals: the reduction still applies, because fewer
pixels never hurt a stuttering loop, but nothing is learned from a window that may hold
nothing but dead time. The protection is bounded on purpose and counted in _intervals_, so
what it buys in wall clock depends on how long they are — four five-second gaps is twenty
seconds, four 400 ms hitches is 1.6. Since a probe also needs its own 1.5-second window and
a representative span before it can pin anything, in practice a burst teaches nothing until
roughly five seconds of continuous dead time: measured with the production step, eight
consecutive 400 ms hitches inside a 60 fps session leave the floor untouched and the pixel
ratio lifts again, while twelve of them (4.8 seconds of stutter) do pin a floor — at which
point the "burst" is a slow regime the manager should be free to learn from.

The rule sees only inter-frame intervals, so two patterns still escape it. A dead period
alternating one-for-one with a single fast frame lands the median between the two phases,
so the dead time is kept as the frame rate and the FPS window mixes it with real render
cost; the manager still adapts there (measured on a 2000/100 ms stream at native 2, the
only rates it ever reports are 0, 10 and 0.5 — all far below the down threshold — and the
pixel ratio does come down), but the rate it reports is not the rate the user perceives.
Two or more fast frames between dead periods fail the other way: the median stays fast, the
dead time is correctly discarded every cycle, and the window accumulates nothing but those
few fast frames — so the 16.7/16.7/400 ms cadence above now evaluates, and reads a healthy
60 fps at about 6.9 perceived.

The Performance panel's FPS row no longer rounds a sub-1 fps rate to `0`, which is its
sentinel for "not rendering at all"; below one frame a second it shows a decimal.
